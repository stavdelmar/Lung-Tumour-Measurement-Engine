LungMarker: results

Research use only. Not for clinical use. No clinical claims. LungMarker helps a researcher measure lung tumours on CT scans faster. An AI finds the tumours and drafts the measurements; the researcher checks and corrects each one, and the results go straight into the lab's Excel sheet, their one source of truth. Built for the Harbor Thoracic Oncology Lab (simulated) and tested on a public set of CT scans: 59 scans from 31 patients, each with one tumour outlined by an expert (RIDER Lung CT).
What works
52 of 59 found
The AI found the tumour the expert had marked in 52 of 59 scans (88%), with no hints.
59 scans, 31 patients
24 of 24 matched
Every tumour found on both of a patient's scans was correctly matched to the earlier scan.
24 patient pairs
3.0 mm vs 2.3 mm
The AI's measurement of a tumour's longest width is typically 3.0 mm from the expert's; two expert measurements of the same tumour differ by 2.3 mm. No tendency to over- or under-measure.
52 tumours, 28 patients (expert: 28 patients)
What fails, or isn't proven yet
7 misses, all central
All 7 missed tumours were in the centre of the chest, next to the heart and main airways (14–52 mm). The app now requires the researcher to check that area before approving; an automatic warning was tried and dropped (too many false alarms).
7 of 59 scans, 4 of 31 patients
19 of 52 off by > 5 mm
AI outlines more than 5 mm from the expert's (13 too small, 6 too large). The researcher confirms every outline before it's saved, but some large misses get no "AI unsure" warning.
52 tumours, 28 patients
Not tested
Faint "ground-glass" nodules: none in the test scans, and the open set that has them (LNDb) needs approval that takes up to 9 days, ending after the deadline. False alarms: the data marks only one tumour per scan. The lab's own scans.
0 ground-glass nodules in the 59 test scans

How I measured the time saved

The honest test is the real researcher timing his work with and without the tool. Because the lab's users are simulated, that wasn't possible. Instead I combined what I could collect, agreed the method with Base44's FDE lead (Sam), and labelled every number as an estimate.

Today's time per incoming scan. Two-thirds of the scans Daniel receives are follow-ups, which take longer than first (baseline) scans, so the average is weighted:
Minutes per scan = ⅓ × baseline + ⅔ × follow-up = ⅓ × 39 + ⅔ × 47.5 ≈ 45 min
Baseline 39 min: Daniel's log (4 scans). Follow-up 40–55 min, midpoint 47.5: Daniel's own step-by-step account (his log: 55 min, 1 scan; using it gives ≈ 50 min, so we use the lower figure). The lab's target, half: about 22 min per scan.

With the tool (estimate). Time with the tool = the tool's own steps (measured: 1–2 active min per scan, 7 developer runs, 2–5 nodules each; 1 more run excluded, see Annex C) + checking each AI draft. Checking wasn't timed: Daniel estimates it takes "about 20–30% of the time" of doing it from scratch, "assuming the draft is generally solid" (Slack, 20 Oct). Applied to his 40–55 min follow-up, that gives about 9–19 min per follow-up (13–19 min at his upper estimate), under half of today's time (20–27.5 min) in every case. The saving holds as long as checking takes less than about 45% of doing it by hand. This rests on Daniel's estimate, not a measurement, and on the drafts being solid: 19 of 52 outlines were more than 5 mm off and central tumours are missed, so those cases take longer.

Limitation and next step. The lab's users are simulated: they answer questions but can't open the tool, so no real reader was timed (checking time is Daniel's estimate) and no real usability test was possible. Daniel approved the flow from screenshots and one external tester (a doctor) tried the tool. The measurement itself is built in: the app times every scan the way Daniel logs it today (the whole review, without long breaks) and fills his "Minutes on scan" column. Next step: Daniel's own timed reviews, with and without the tool, on a small set of the lab's scans, to replace his estimate with a measurement.

Annex A · Method

  • Data: RIDER Lung CT (public, The Cancer Imaging Archive): 31 patients, each scanned twice about 15 minutes apart; 59 scans with an expert outline of one tumour each (RIDER-LungCT-Seg). Tumours 13–93 mm (median 43 mm), mostly solid (53 solid, 6 part-solid), no ground-glass. The lab measures nodules from 4 mm, so these are larger and clearer than its usual cases.
  • How the AI ran: the whole pipeline with no hint: lobes, then nodule finding (TotalSegmentator lung_nodules, full settings, free Colab T4 GPU, median 49 s per scan), then outline and measurement, then matching between each patient's two scans. The finding model was not trained on RIDER.
  • How quality was measured: the AI's outline and the expert's outline of the same tumour are measured the same way, the way Daniel measures: longest width on the slice where the tumour is largest, the longest perpendicular width on that slice, and volume from the 3D outline. Error = AI minus expert, per tumour; we report the median of the absolute error and the median of the signed error (bias).
  • Expert noise (the yardstick): the expert outlined the same tumour on both of a patient's scans, taken 15 minutes apart. The median difference between those two outlines (28 patients) is 2.3 mm for longest width and 17% for volume. The AI is "within expert noise" when its error is no larger.
  • Outline setting: the model's core plus a rim scaled to the core's size (the shipped setting). It was chosen by comparing the model's own outline, MedSAM 2 and four rim variants on these same 52 tumours, so the quality numbers are somewhat optimistic.
  • What this measures: the AI's first draft. The researcher's confirmed values (what goes into the sheet) were not measured.
